Closures, from what they compile to. Section 5 is why serde is built
out of visitors instead, and section 6 counts what the crate writes.

1. A closure is a struct the compiler wrote
-------------------------------------------
  closure                            words
  || 29             (no capture)         0
  || name.len()     (&String)            1
  move || name.as_ptr() (String)         3
  String (for comparison)                3

borrowing closure read the original: yes
moved closure holds the same buffer:  yes

The sizes are the whole lesson. A closure with nothing to remember is
zero bytes — it exists only in the type system, and calling it compiles
to calling a function. Capturing by reference costs one pointer.
Capturing by value costs whatever the value costs, and the `String`
went in the way it goes anywhere else: three words copied, the same
heap buffer, no allocation. What varies is not how closures work; it is
what you asked to be remembered.

Nothing here is magic syntax. `|n| n + captured` is a struct with one
field and one method, and the only reason you cannot name its type is
that the compiler never gave it a name to say.

2. Three traits, because there are three receivers
--------------------------------------------------
  trait     receiver     may                      callable
  FnOnce    self         move its captures out    once
  FnMut     &mut self    mutate its captures      many, exclusively
  Fn        &self        read its captures        many, shared

Fn:     map_three(|n| n + offset) -> [101, 102, 103]
FnMut:  log after two calls        -> ["ALPHA", "BETA"]
FnOnce: finish(move || owned)      -> "moved out of the closure"

one closure, three bounds: Fn [2, 4, 6]  FnMut [2]  FnOnce "42"

The three traits are not three kinds of closure. They are three
questions about the one method: does calling it need to consume the
struct, mutate it, or only read it? So they nest — `Fn: FnMut` and
`FnMut: FnOnce` — and every closure implements as many of them as its
body allows. `|n| n * 2` implements all three, which is why `plain`
went into all three functions above.

The direction matters when you are the one writing the bound. Ask for
`FnOnce` and you may call it once but almost anything may be passed;
ask for `Fn` and you may call it whenever you like, including from
several threads, but a closure that counts something is refused. Take
the weakest bound that does the job — `Option::unwrap_or_else` asks for
`FnOnce` because it calls at most once, and `Iterator::map` asks for
`FnMut` because it calls repeatedly but never concurrently.

3. Every closure is its own type — hence Box<dyn Fn>
----------------------------------------------------
add_one and also_add_one agree on 10: yes
through Vec<Box<dyn Fn>> on 10:       [11, 11, 30]

  value                              words
  add_one (the closure itself)           0
  scaled  (captures one usize)           1
  fn(u32) -> u32 pointer                 1
  Box<dyn Fn(u32) -> u32>                2
  PhantomData<u32>                       0

A `Box<dyn Fn>` is two words wide whatever it holds: a pointer to the
closure and a pointer to its vtable. That is the cost of putting
different closures in one `Vec` — an allocation each and an indirect
call — and the reason `impl Fn` is the default choice. A generic
`F: Fn(u32) -> u32` is compiled once per closure type, with the body
inlined and the zero-sized ones vanishing entirely, which is unit 11's
monomorphization arriving from the other direction: there, one macro
wrote many impls; here, one function becomes many machine functions.

The two `add_one` closures above are a special case worth knowing: a
closure that captures nothing has nothing to carry, so it coerces to a
plain `fn(u32) -> u32` and several of them can share one `Vec` after
all. Give either of them a capture and that door closes — which is the
`closure_types_differ` case in `examples/compile_fail`, where rustc
says it plainly: no two closures, even if identical, have the same
type.

`PhantomData` measuring zero next to a non-capturing closure is not a
coincidence. Both are types carrying no data, and serde uses the first
exactly where you would use the second — `impl DeserializeSeed for
PhantomData<T>` is the seed that captured nothing.

4. What is captured is decided one variable at a time
-----------------------------------------------------
closure holds record.name; record.hits still mutable: yes (now 1)
move || over a u32: closure reached (2, 4), outer stayed 1
counter(41) returned a closure: 42 43

Three separate things people call "capture". *What* is captured is
whatever the body names — `record.name`, not all of `record`, which is
an edition 2021 change and the fix for closures that used to lock a
whole struct. *How* it is captured is the weakest mode the body needs:
shared, then mutable, then by value. `move` overrides only that second
question, forcing by-value, and it is what makes a closure that
outlives its scope possible at all — `counter` returns something that
still works after every local in it is gone, because the local moved
into the struct that was returned.

And `move` on a `Copy` type is a copy. The closure started from the
same 1 the outer variable held, reached 4 across two calls, and the
outer variable never moved. The keyword does not mean "share";
it means "take a value of your own", which for `u32` is a duplicate
and for `String` is the only copy there is.

5. A closure has one signature — serde needs twenty-seven
---------------------------------------------------------
drive(Str) -> "field: a string of 5 bytes"
drive(Num) -> "field: the number 229"

size of Describe, captures included: 3 words

Try to write `drive` taking a closure and the design collapses at the
first line: `|v| ...` takes one type of argument, and the whole problem
is that the driver does not know which type it has until it has looked.
Passing two closures would work for two cases and not for twenty-seven,
and they could not share captured state without cloning it.

So `Visitor` is the closure with twenty-seven entry points —
`visit_bool(self, bool)`, `visit_str(self, &str)`, `visit_seq(self,
impl SeqAccess)`, and the rest — every one taking `self` by value,
because exactly one of them will ever run. That is `FnOnce`, written
out longhand and multiplied by the data model.

`DeserializeSeed` is the other half of the analogy and closer still:
`fn deserialize(self, deserializer: D)` is a `FnOnce` that carries
captured state into the deserialization and returns a value. Its impl
for `PhantomData<T>` is the one that captured nothing — zero words,
same as `|| 29` in section 1 — which is why the common case costs
nothing to pass around.

The price is honest: a visitor is thirty lines where a closure is one.
`serde_derive` exists to write those thirty lines, and that is most of
what it does.

6. The thirteen closures in serde_core, and their bounds
--------------------------------------------------------
  file           n  closure                                    bound
  de/impls.rs    6  map_err(|_| Error::invalid_value(..))      FnOnce
  de/impls.rs    2  map(|vec| ..) / map(|()| ..)               FnOnce
  de/impls.rs    1  ok_or_else(|| Error::custom(..))           FnOnce
  de/impls.rs    2  |(ip, port)| SocketAddrV4::new(..)         FnOnce
  ser/mod.rs     2  try_for_each(|item| serialize_element(..)) FnMut
                13  in 12,037 lines                            no Fn at all

Thirteen closures in two files, and the shape of the list is the
point. Eleven are `FnOnce` positions — an error constructor on a path
that has already failed, run at most once. Two are `FnMut`, both of
them the same line in `collect_seq` and `collect_map`, capturing the
serializer mutably to feed it one element at a time. None is an `Fn`.

Two of them are worth a second look: the `|(ip, port)|` pair are
arguments to `parse_socket_impl!`, passed through the macro as `$new:
expr` and landing in `.map($new)` inside the generated impl. A closure
crossing a macro boundary is unit 11 and this unit meeting — and they
capture nothing, so they are constructors that could have been function
pointers, written as closures because the tuple pattern is neater.

The number that is not in the table is the one that matters most:
serde_core declares zero `Fn`, `FnMut` or `FnOnce` bounds of its own,
and zero `dyn Fn`. It never asks you for a closure. Where a library
built around callbacks would take one, this crate takes a `Serialize`,
a `Visitor`, or a `DeserializeSeed` — a named type with named methods,
which can have twenty-seven of them, can be implemented for a type you
do not own, and can be generated by a derive macro. Closures buy
brevity at one call site. Traits buy everything this crate needed.
