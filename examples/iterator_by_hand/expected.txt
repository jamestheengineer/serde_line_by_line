The iterator protocol, from the one method it actually requires.
Sections 4, 5 and 6 are the crate's three iterator lessons, reduced
to code that runs.

1. The trait is one method — everything else is provided
--------------------------------------------------------
collect()          ["alpha", "beta", "gamma"]
count()            3
map + max_by_key   Some("gamma")
enumerate + last   Some((2, "gamma"))

One `next` was written. `collect`, `count`, `max_by_key`, `enumerate`
and `last` all came with the trait, and every one of them is defined in
terms of calling `next` until it returns `None`. `for f in it` is not
syntax for a collection — it is `IntoIterator::into_iter` followed by a
loop over `next`, which is why it works on anything that implements
this and on nothing that does not.

2. Nothing happens until someone calls next()
---------------------------------------------
  built map(...).filter(...)         closure ran 0 times
  one next() -> Some(1)              closure ran 1 time
  find(|sq| *sq > 8) -> Some(9)      closure ran 3 times of 8

Building the chain ran nothing: `map` and `filter` return *values* —
`Map<Iter<u64>, {closure}>` and a `Filter` wrapped around it — that
hold the source and the closure and do no work. Each `next` on the
outer one calls `next` on the inner one, exactly as often as it must.
`find` stopped at the third element because the answer was known then;
nothing after it was squared. This is why a chain of ten adapters over
a million items allocates nothing and touches only what it needs, and
why forgetting to consume one is a warning rather than a slow program.

3. size_hint — a claim the caller allocates against
---------------------------------------------------
  slice.iter()                 (5, Some(5))
  .map(square)                 (5, Some(5))
  .filter(is_odd)              (0, Some(5))
  .chain(slice.iter())         (10, Some(10))
  Fields::new(record)          (0, None)

collect() from map:    5 items, capacity 5
collect() from filter: 5 items, capacity 8

Same five values both times. `map` cannot change how many there are,
so it forwards the exact hint and `collect` allocates once, for five.
`filter` can only say "between none and five", `collect` believes the
lower bound, and the `Vec` reaches five by doubling — the capacity
above is the growth showing. (Capacities are this std's behaviour, not
a promise.) `Fields` reports the default `(0, None)`, because the
trait's provided `size_hint` is the honest answer for anything that has
to look at the data to know.

serde_core's `private/size_hint.rs` is this exact question at the other
end of the pipe. `from_bounds` returns `Some` only when the two bounds
agree — "at least 3, at most 100" is discarded rather than guessed at —
and `cautious` then caps whatever survives at one megabyte's worth of
elements, because past that point the number came from the input file
and a four-byte header should not be able to ask for a gigabyte.

4. After None, the trait promises nothing — hence Fuse
------------------------------------------------------
five next() calls, unfused: [Some(1), Some(2), None, Some(99), None]
five next() calls, fused:   [Some(1), Some(2), None, None, None]
the 99 came back after None:  yes
what a `for` loop would see: [1, 2]

The `for` loop never notices, because it stops at the first `None` and
never asks again. That is what makes this a good bug: it is invisible
to the common case and only appears when the loop is somewhere else.

Which is exactly serde's situation. In `SeqDeserializer` the loop
belongs to the `Visitor`, and a visitor may call `next_element_seed`
as many times as it likes, including past the end — then `end()` calls
`count()` on the same iterator afterwards to check the length. A
resurrecting iterator would make those two disagree. So the field is
typed `iter: iter::Fuse<I>`, not `I`: `Fuse` latches a flag the first
time it sees `None` and returns `None` forever after, which is the
promise the trait declines to make. It is one word in a struct
definition and it is load-bearing.

5. Two Options as a state machine, not a cursor
-----------------------------------------------
  call     yields           size_hint
  next()   Some("version")  Some(2)
  next()   Some("1.0.229")  Some(1)
  next()   None             Some(0)
  next()   None             Some(0)

`take` moves the value out and leaves `None` behind, so the sequence
key, value, end, end is a consequence of the two fields rather than
something a counter has to be kept in step with. An index would need a
`match` with arms that cannot happen; here the impossible states are
unrepresentable, and the borrow checker is what verifies nothing is
handed out twice — you cannot `take` from a `None` and get a value.
`size_hint` is derived from the same two fields, which is why it can
always return `Some` and the caller in `deserialize_seq` can `unwrap`
it without a comment apologising.

6. Asking twice, then stopping
------------------------------
  input       len==1  count==1  two nexts
  "a"            yes       yes        yes
  "é"             no       yes        yes
  ""              no        no         no
  "ab"            no        no         no
  "€10"           no        no         no

on a 10,000-character string, same answer (yes), different cost:
  chars().count() == 1   visited 10000 characters
  two nexts              visited 2 characters

The third cautionary tale, and the smallest. `visit_str` for `char`
has to decide whether the input holds exactly one character, and the
two obvious spellings are each wrong in their own way: `len() == 1`
asks about bytes and rejects every accented letter, and
`chars().count() == 1` walks the whole thing — ten thousand characters
above to answer a question about the first two, and a ten-megabyte
input decoded end to end before it says no.

Taking two items and matching on the pair costs two `next` calls
whatever the input is, and reads as the question rather than as a
proxy for it. That is the shape worth stealing: when you want to know
how many items there are only up to some small number, ask for that
many and look at what came back. Anything else — empty, or two or more
— falls through to one error carrying the string that was there.

7. Why there are no closures here to look at
--------------------------------------------
A closure has one call signature. `|s: &str| ...` takes a `&str` and
nothing else, and that is the whole reason serde is built out of
visitors instead. A `Visitor` is what you would write if a closure
could have twenty-seven entry points with twenty-seven different
argument types — `visit_bool(bool)`, `visit_u64(u64)`,
`visit_str(&str)`, `visit_seq(impl SeqAccess)` — and be handed to a
`Deserializer` that picks one after it has seen the data.

So the trade is visible: application code would pass a closure and take
one shape of input; serde passes a trait object's worth of methods and
takes any of them. The cost is that a visitor is thirty lines and a
closure is one, which is what `serde_derive` exists to write for you.
The `closure_kinds` example is the other half of this unit, and it is
where `Fn`, `FnMut` and `FnOnce` are actually taught, because there is
nothing in this crate to point at for them.
